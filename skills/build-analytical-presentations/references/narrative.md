## Central question and reader promise

Start with a question whose answer matters to the intended audience. A subject alone is too broad. "Service queues" is a subject. "Which change can reduce waiting without increasing rework?" is a useful question.

The reader promise defines what the presentation will help someone understand or decide. It should also bound the work. A review of one pilot cannot promise a conclusion about every operating site.

A long presentation may benefit from an orientation slide: what is covered, what prior knowledge is assumed, how the sections fit, and what is deliberately outside scope. A short briefing usually needs only a subtitle or opening sentence. Avoid introductory ceremony that delays an urgent decision.

## Section architecture

Construct sections around dependencies in the argument. A definition should arrive before a comparison that depends on it. A result should be evaluated against criteria that the audience has already seen.

Useful architectures include:

| Architecture | Sequence | Use when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Explanation | Question, essentials, mechanism, examples, constraints, synthesis | The audience needs a model before an implication makes sense |
| Recommendation | Recommendation, decision criteria, evidence, alternatives, risks, ask | A bounded choice is the main purpose |
| Diagnosis | Observed problem, process map, evidence, competing explanations, test, response | The cause is uncertain or contested |
| Technical argument | Requirement, design, method, result, limitation, acceptance or next test | The audience must assess whether something works |
| Change briefing | Baseline, what changed, why it matters, implications, response | The audience already knows the background |

These are patterns to adapt. Combine or omit stages when the brief warrants it. Do not manufacture a crisis, a personal story, or a grand market claim to make routine work sound consequential.

## Slide sequences and transitions

A useful explanatory sequence often moves through:

1. Establish a condition or concept.
2. Show the relationship or mechanism.
3. Quantify it or examine an example.
4. Explain the consequence and its limits.

This can occupy one slide or several. Treat the sequence as a reasoning unit, not a fixed four-slide template.

Consider this synthetic chain:

- The observed waiting time has increased.
- Requests converge at a shared verification step.
- That step receives more work than it can process during the afternoon peak.
- A trial should test whether moving verification earlier reduces the queue.

Each step earns the next. The last statement is a proposal, not proof that the trial will work.

At section boundaries, resolve the previous question and introduce the next dependency. For example: "The constraint is now clear. The next question is whether either option can remove it within the available budget." The transition can appear in the last sentence, the next headline, or a section opener. It does not require a dedicated slide.

Avoid a disconnected run of "overview", "benefits", "opportunities", and "next steps". Those labels describe document furniture, not the argument.

## Headline-only test

Read the proposed headlines in order. A reader should be able to recognise the argument's progression, even if some necessary labels are topics rather than assertions.

Diagnose these problems:

- Repetition: several headlines say that the issue matters without advancing understanding.
- Missing premise: a conclusion appears before its definition or evidence.
- Missing consequence: detailed facts never resolve into an implication.
- Contradiction: an early headline sounds certain while later evidence remains provisional.
- Broken ending: the presentation finishes with a recap but never resolves its decision.

Repair the sequence before decorating slides.

## Pacing and synthesis

Alternate explanation and evidence according to cognitive demand. A dense mechanism may need a simple framing slide before it and an application afterwards. A straightforward comparison rarely needs three separate introductory slides.

Use section openers when the audience is moving to a materially different question or level of abstraction. Too many dividers fragment a short deck. Too few landmarks make a long document hard to navigate.

Synthesis should integrate, not repeat every bullet. State what the reader can now conclude, how strong that conclusion is, and what remains unresolved. Return to the questions that organised the presentation. If the material changes the initial framing, explain why.

For a decision deck, close with the action, amount or scope, owner, timing, and conditions that actually require agreement. For a technical review, close with the criterion met or missed and the next evidence needed. For teaching, close with the model and its boundaries rather than forcing a commercial ask.

## Revising an existing deck

Identify the current argument, supplied constraints, and valuable existing material. Preserve user changes and comments. Distinguish a local wording problem from a structural problem.

For a local edit, make the local edit. For a substantial rewrite, map existing slides to the argument, remove duplication, supply missing links from authorised evidence, and preserve an appendix where it has a clear purpose. Explain material omissions or reordered conclusions in the handoff.
