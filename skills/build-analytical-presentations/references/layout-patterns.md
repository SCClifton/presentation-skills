Use this as a vocabulary, not a catalogue to exhaust. Several patterns can share the same visual grid. Choose based on the information relationship, audience, and delivery setting. Density limits below are editing signals, not universal word counts.

## 1. Cover and reader brief

**Use:** Establish what the presentation addresses and what it will do for the reader.

**Arrangement:** A concise title, a useful subtitle or outcome, and necessary date or status. In a long independent-reading deck, follow with a short reader brief explaining the purpose, assumptions, boundaries, and route through the material.

**Can vary:** A short briefing can combine the cover, recommendation, and reader promise. A supplied company cover takes precedence.

**Density signal:** If the reader brief becomes a miniature report, retain the purpose and boundaries and move supporting background into the appropriate section.

**Failure:** A broad title promises more than the evidence supports, or three introduction slides delay the actual question.

## 2. Contents and navigation

**Use:** Help someone navigate a presentation long enough to have distinct chapters.

**Arrangement:** Meaningful section names in reading order, with accurate page numbers or links where useful. A small section label can locate content slides. A repeated progress map can show where a chapter fits when the system is genuinely complex.

**Can vary:** Replace a contents slide with a short agenda in a meeting. Omit it from a short deck that already has a clear flow.

**Density signal:** An agenda listing every slide is too granular. Group by the questions being answered.

**Failure:** Navigation labels change between the contents and the slides, or stale page references point to the wrong section.

## 3. Section opener

**Use:** Signal a change in question, subject, or level of abstraction.

**Arrangement:** A short chapter label and a larger title or framing question. Use generous space. A subtitle can establish the consequence of the next section.

**Can vary:** A different background, an image, or simple typography can mark the boundary within the current brand. No particular visual treatment is required.

**Density signal:** Avoid embedding the entire section summary on the divider. Give substantive explanation its own content slide.

**Failure:** A divider after every minor point makes the narrative feel fragmented.

## 4. Narrative or bridge

**Use:** Explain a transition, a causal argument, or a concept that does not need a diagram.

**Arrangement:** One clear reading column containing a few short connected paragraphs, or a short statement with one supporting explanation. Leave enough space for the idea to register. Place emphasis on the essential relationship or conclusion.

**Can vary:** Put a title above the text or let a deliberately composed statement carry the page. Use a bridge between two evidence-heavy sections when the relationship needs explanation.

**Density signal:** If the paragraphs introduce independent claims requiring different evidence, split them. Do not fill the empty half of the slide with irrelevant decoration.

**Failure:** A poster slogan has no analytical content, or a full essay has been pasted into a slide without hierarchy.

## 5. Explanation beside evidence

**Use:** Pair an interpretation with a chart, diagram, photograph, map, or compact table that supports it.

**Arrangement:** A headline above two unequal columns when needed. One column explains the condition, mechanism, or implication. The other contains the dominant evidence object. Align both columns to a coherent grid. Keep the source close to the object.

**Can vary:** Reverse the columns when reading order calls for it. Let the evidence occupy more space when labels need room. Text and image do not need equal visual weight.

**Density signal:** If each column requires tiny text, give the evidence a whole slide or divide the argument into an explanation and a result.

**Failure:** The prose claims something absent from the chart, or both columns repeat the same information word for word.

## 6. Dominant chart

**Use:** Let one quantitative relationship carry the slide.

**Arrangement:** A finding or question above a large chart. Label important values directly, identify units and time windows, and add only the annotation necessary to explain the main comparison. Use the chart's visual hierarchy to guide attention.

**Can vary:** Use small multiples for genuinely comparable cases. Add a compact implication beside or below the chart if the audience needs help interpreting it.

**Density signal:** Remove redundant legends, gridlines, and data labels before reducing label size. If multiple messages compete, separate the views.

**Failure:** An attractive chart answers a different question, a truncated scale distorts a comparison, or a source note cannot be read at the intended size.

## 7. Mechanism or layered system

**Use:** Explain how components, stages, dependencies, or feedback fit together.

**Arrangement:** Organise around the actual relationship: a flow for a sequence, layers for dependency, a tree for hierarchy, or a loop for feedback. Name the components and label important arrows. Explain what moves through the system and what is constrained.

**Can vary:** Build the model across slides, first showing the simple structure and then the needed detail. Keep component names and locations stable. Use a side explanation for the consequence rather than repeating every box label.

**Density signal:** If the reader must trace crossing arrows across the whole slide, split the system into an overview and focused details. Include enough context in each detail to preserve orientation.

**Failure:** Decorative connected boxes imply a process that does not exist, or arrow direction changes the meaning of the system.

## 8. Comparison or option matrix

**Use:** Compare a small set of alternatives against the same criteria.

**Arrangement:** Parallel columns or rows, consistent units and criteria, and a visible basis for any recommendation. Distinguish measured facts, estimates, and judgements. Include the status quo when it is a real choice.

**Can vary:** Use paired diagrams for a structural comparison or a table for several criteria. A recommendation can sit above the comparison instead of occupying a separate slide.

**Density signal:** Too many options or criteria obscure the choice. Put the decisive criteria in the main sequence and fuller detail in backup material if allowed.

**Failure:** The preferred option gets favourable criteria or a different denominator, or unsupported scores create false precision.

## 9. Timeline or staged progression

**Use:** Explain events, process maturity, a plan, or a sequence of dependencies.

**Arrangement:** Ordered stages with short labels and relevant dates or duration. Distinguish observed events from proposed milestones. Use proportional spacing only when position represents elapsed time; otherwise label the sequence as schematic.

**Can vary:** A vertical timeline suits longer descriptions. A step diagram may communicate dependencies better than a calendar.

**Density signal:** If a timeline becomes an unreadable row of cards, select material events or use multiple aligned views.

**Failure:** A proposed milestone looks completed, equal spacing implies equal time, or the slide lists events without explaining why they matter.

## 10. Structured table

**Use:** Let the reader compare exact values, categories, requirements, or exceptions.

**Arrangement:** Specific headers, comparable entries, consistent units, clear alignment, and restrained rules or shading. Give the row labels enough width. Use a short interpretation when the implication is not obvious.

**Can vary:** Group related rows, divide a large table, or highlight a material exception. Keep detailed lookup tables in an appendix when the main decision does not require reading every cell.

**Density signal:** If a cell needs a paragraph or the headers cannot remain legible, simplify the columns or split the table.

**Failure:** A dense copied spreadsheet replaces a presentation argument, or rounding hides a material difference.

## 11. Annotated example

**Use:** Make an abstract concept concrete through one real or explicitly fictional case.

**Arrangement:** A short setup, a dominant example, and a few annotations tied to specific features. Explain which feature illustrates the general mechanism and where the comparison stops.

**Can vary:** Use a photograph, record excerpt, process trace, mock interface, or simple worked calculation. Use native callouts when editing matters.

**Density signal:** If annotations explain several unrelated concepts, choose one focus or use more than one slide.

**Failure:** The example becomes a story detached from the argument, confidential material is exposed to the wrong audience, or an illustration is treated as representative evidence.

## 12. Synthesis and decision

**Use:** Integrate a section or resolve the presentation's promise.

**Arrangement:** State the answer, the evidence boundary, and the implication. For a decision, make the action and conditions explicit. A short list, answer table, or compact decision block is sufficient when well written.

**Can vary:** Revisit the opening questions, compare what was expected with what was learned, or show the next validation step. A section recap can also locate the next chapter in the larger model.

**Density signal:** If the synthesis repeats every prior point, keep only what changes the audience's understanding or action.

**Failure:** A generic "thank you" replaces the needed ask, or the conclusion introduces a new unsupported premise.
