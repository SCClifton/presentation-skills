## Headline choices

A headline can state a finding, pose a question, or locate the reader. Choose consciously.

| Type | Example | What the body must do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Finding | Afternoon demand exceeds verification capacity | Show the demand and capacity comparison |
| Question | Where does the queue form? | Identify the location from evidence |
| Topic | Verification workflow | Explain the workflow or introduce the section |
| Decision | Approve a two-week verification trial | Specify scope, evidence, risk, and the requested agreement |

All examples here are fictional. Do not use their numbers as real evidence.

Prefer a finding headline for a slide whose evidence supports one. A concise topic title can be appropriate for a mechanism diagram, definition, or section opener. Do not convert an uncertain hypothesis into a confident headline merely to make the presentation sound decisive.

Check whether the headline is broader than the chart. A result measured at one desk over five days cannot establish an organisation-wide effect. Avoid headlines containing a conclusion that depends on an unshown calculation.

Shorten titles by removing introductory padding and duplicate context, not by deleting qualifiers that change the claim. Let the subtitle carry the measurement window when the title otherwise becomes cumbersome.

## Explanatory prose and emphasis

Use connected sentences when one fact explains another. A useful paragraph establishes a condition, explains its consequence, and stops before moving to a different topic.

Weak:

> Verification. High demand. Queue challenges. Need for optimisation.

Better:

> Requests arrive faster than the verification desk can complete them during the afternoon peak. The queue grows even though the daily total remains within overall capacity.

Bullets work well for alternatives, criteria, prerequisites, and steps. Make their grammar parallel. Do not use a bullet to conceal a paragraph that needs editing.

Use emphasis to identify a key phrase, an exception, or a conclusion. If everything is highlighted, nothing has priority. Give categories stable visual treatment within the deck, but let the organisation's template determine its actual colours.

Avoid writing that merely announces importance: "a transformative opportunity", "unlocking potential", "best-in-class", or "a new era". Replace it with a specific change, mechanism, constraint, or decision.

## Quantities and evidence

For a quantitative claim, establish the quantity, unit, time window, population, and comparison. Add the denominator for a rate and identify whether values are averages, medians, peaks, totals, or estimates.

Keep separate:

- Observation: what the data or record shows.
- Source statement: what a cited party claims.
- Calculation: what follows from a stated method and inputs.
- Interpretation: what the evidence suggests.
- Scenario: what would happen under stated assumptions.
- Proposal: what the presentation asks someone to do.

Do not place these labels on every sentence if the distinction is already clear. Make the distinction visible where a reader could otherwise mistake one for another.

For a chart, preserve consistent units, comparable time windows, legible axes, and honest scale. Show a baseline and uncertainty when they affect interpretation. Distinguish percentage change from percentage-point change. A before-and-after result does not establish causation without an appropriate design.

Put a compact source note near the evidence. Include enough information in the notes or references for another person to find the underlying record. Never use a vague organisation name as though it independently substantiates the whole slide. Check whether a source supports the particular number, time period, and interpretation being claimed.

If the source is confidential, use the reference convention authorised for the current audience. Do not fetch external information or expose internal material merely to complete a citation field.

## Caveats and uncertainty

Place a caveat close to the claim it qualifies. A generic final disclaimer does not repair an overstated headline. Separate uncertain magnitude from uncertain direction, and an untested proposal from an observed result.

If evidence is missing, state what is missing and what decision can still be made. Use an explicit placeholder only in a clearly labelled draft. Never invent numbers to make a chart look complete.

Use the weakest claim that remains useful and supported. "The pilot recorded fewer repeat requests" can be justified when "the process permanently eliminates rework" cannot.

## Editing examples

### Vague conclusion

Before: "Significant operational improvement opportunities exist."

After: "The process study attributes 14 minutes of waiting to verification."

Why: the revision identifies the step, measured quantity, and scope. It is suitable only when those measurements actually exist.

### Unsupported causality

Before: "The new form reduced repeat requests by 20%."

After: "Repeat requests were 20% lower during the form trial; other process changes may also have contributed."

Why: the revision preserves the observation while qualifying causal attribution.

### Compressed jargon

Before: "Deploy a scalable intervention to optimise cross-functional throughput."

After: "Trial earlier verification so complete requests reach the service team before the afternoon peak."

Why: the revision names the action and the proposed mechanism. It remains a proposal.

### Missing denominator

Before: "Failures fell from 8 to 4."

After: "Failed checks fell from 8 of 100 requests to 4 of 100 requests in the two observed weeks."

Why: the revision establishes the denominator and observation window. If the denominators differ, show the two rates rather than implying equal volumes.

### Overloaded heading

Before: "A review of how the current verification process is creating waiting time and what this means for future service improvement."

After: "Verification is the main measured source of waiting."

Subtitle: "Five-day sample, one service desk."

Why: the main conclusion becomes scannable without losing its evidence boundary.
