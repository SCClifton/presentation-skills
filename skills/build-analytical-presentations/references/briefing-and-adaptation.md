# Briefing and adaptation

## Resolve the actual job

The same evidence can support several different presentations. Decide what the audience needs to do with it before choosing a sequence.

| Mode | Audience's task | Good opening | Useful ending |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Teaching | Build a usable mental model | A concrete question and a bounded promise | An explanation the reader can apply, including its limits |
| Executive briefing | Understand what changed and what matters | The material change and its consequence | Implications, unresolved issues, and next attention points |
| Decision | Choose or authorise a course of action | The recommendation, decision, and stakes | The exact ask, conditions, owner, and next checkpoint |
| Technical review | Evaluate whether a method or result meets a need | The requirement and acceptance criteria | Results, residual risk, and the next validation step |
| Progress review | Distinguish completed work from remaining work | Status against the agreed objective | Next milestones, dependencies, and decisions needed |

Do not force a teaching introduction into a decision meeting where participants already know the background. Do not assume technical readers share every acronym or the history of the project.

## Build a compact brief

Capture the audience, desired outcome, central message, evidence boundary, delivery setting, and required format. For example:

> The service manager must choose a bounded queue-reduction trial. Readers know the current process but have not seen the measurements. Recommend a two-week trial, show the measured bottleneck and alternative explanations, and define success before requesting approval.

This is a working aid, not a mandatory extra document. Reuse a supplied brief rather than making the user repeat it.

## Change delivery settings deliberately

### Live presentation

The speaker can supply context, but the slide must still communicate its main relationship. Prefer fewer concurrent comparisons and larger labels. Place detailed definitions and qualification in notes or backup slides when they are unnecessary to the immediate decision. Do not hide a material caveat only in notes.

Test how much the audience can understand while also listening. A slide with only a short phrase may depend on the speaker; a slide containing the complete spoken paragraph can compete with the speaker. Choose the amount of visible text for the job.

### Independent reading

Include the connective explanation that a speaker would otherwise provide. Short paragraphs can be more useful than fragments. Define terms, identify units and time periods, label diagrams directly, and provide sources that the reader can locate. Ensure conclusions still follow when no one narrates the transitions.

### Both

Build a concise main sequence and put deeper explanation in notes or a clearly identified appendix. If the same file must do both jobs, prioritise the primary setting stated by the user. Avoid filling every slide to the maximum density merely because it might be emailed later.

## Adapt to an organisation

Treat the organisation's identity as task input. Use its approved template, terminology, required footer and information-handling rules. Respect supplied definitions of readiness, delivery, acceptance, and approval.

Keep these separate:

- General presentation method: argument, explanation, evidence, and arrangement.
- Current task content: the organisation's claims, facts, people, products, and decisions.
- Current task appearance: its brand and presentation template.

Never import facts or terminology from an unrelated client or employer to fill a gap. When changing organisations, re-brief the audience and decision rather than replacing a logo on an otherwise unchanged argument.

## Compress without breaking the argument

Identify which slides establish a premise, explain a mechanism, supply decisive evidence, test an alternative, or resolve the ask. Retain the dependencies needed for the conclusion. Remove repetition and optional illustration before removing the evidence boundary.

For a short briefing, the reader promise can be one sentence and the conclusion can be the opening recommendation revisited with conditions. A long primer may need chapters, explicit definitions, recurring examples, and section synthesis. There is no preferred universal slide count.

If a required length cannot hold all necessary material, use a main sequence plus an appendix when allowed. Otherwise prioritise and disclose the omitted material instead of shrinking text until it is unreadable.
